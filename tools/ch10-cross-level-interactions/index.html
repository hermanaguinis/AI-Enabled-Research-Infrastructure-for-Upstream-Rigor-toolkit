Phase 1 · Design Recommendation 1

Define the cross-level interaction effect

Do: identify the cross-level interaction as γ11, the coefficient on the product of the L1 and L2 predictors in the combined equation. Why: people routinely mistake slope variance (τ11) for the interaction — but τ11 only tells you a moderator search is warranted; it never identifies which moderator matters.

Two ways to write the same effect

γ11 vs. τ11 — the crucial distinction

γ11 — the interaction effect. A single number (a fixed effect). It answers: does leadership climate explain the differences in the LMX–empowerment slope? Positive γ11 → the slope is steeper in groups with higher W.
τ11 — slope variance. Tells you only that slopes differ across groups — that heterogeneity exists. Like heterogeneity in meta-analysis, it signals that searching for a moderator is warranted, but it is not the interaction. Raudenbush & Bryk call γ11 the "distributive effect."

Self-check

Q1. A reviewer says "your τ11 is significant, so you've shown the cross-level interaction." Are they right?

Yes — significant slope variance is the interaction effect.
No — τ11 shows slopes vary, but the interaction is γ11, the product-term coefficient that identifies which L2 variable explains that variance.

Q2. In the combined equation, which term carries the cross-level interaction?

γ01(Wj − W̄) — the L2 main effect.
γ11(Xij − X̄j)(Wj − W̄) — the product of the centered L1 and L2 predictors.
u1j(Xij − X̄j) — the random slope term.
Phase 1 · Design Recommendation 2

Statistical power & design decisions

Do: plan sample sizes so the study can actually detect the interaction, using the Mathieu et al. (2012) Monte-Carlo approach. Why: published power for cross-level interactions averages only ~.40 at α=.05 (and ~.22 at α=.01) — worse than a coin flip. Rules of thumb like "30/30" or "30/50" should be abandoned.

What drives power here (from the article)

Power to detect a cross-level interaction is driven mainly by three things: the magnitude of the interaction (γ11), the variance of L1 slopes across groups (τ11), and the L1 and L2 sample sizes. You usually can't control the first two, but you can plan the sample. Critically, L1 sample size carries a roughly 3:2 premium over L2 sample size for detecting the interaction — while L2 size matters most for cross-level direct effects. Match your allocation to which effects you most need to detect.

Phase 1 · Design Recommendation 3

Continuous vs. categorical Level-2 moderators

Do: decide your L2 moderator's type up front, because it changes how γ11 is interpreted. Why: a continuous W gives a "per-unit slope change"; a categorical W gives a "difference in slopes between groups." Same equation, different sentence.

Phase 2–3 · Conduct & Analyze Model building

The four-step model-building walkthrough

Do: build the model in four steps — Null → Random-Intercept Fixed-Slope → Random-Intercept Random-Slope → Cross-Level Interaction. Why: each step answers a distinct question and sets up the next; skipping steps hides where the variance lives.

FIML or REML?

FIML — full information ML. Deviance reflects both fixed coefficients and variance components. Use it to compare models that differ in fixed effects (e.g., Step 2 vs Step 4) and for the deviance tests in Table 1.
REML — restricted ML. Deviance reflects variance components only; gives less biased variance estimates. Use it for final variance estimates and it is also valid for testing whether τ11 is nonzero.

Step 1 — Null model + ICC

Yij = γ00 + u0j + rij

No predictors yet — intercepts are allowed to vary across teams. Then compute the ICC.

Interpretation: ICC is the proportion of outcome variance between groups (and the expected correlation of two cases in one group). ICC near 0 → single-level OLS may suffice. ICC > 0, even ~.10, justifies multilevel modeling; in management studies ICCs typically run .15–.30.

Step 2 — Random-Intercept Fixed-Slope (RIFSM)

Yij = γ00 + γ10(Xij−X̄j) + γ01(Wj−W̄) + u0j + rij

Adds the L1 predictor and the L2 predictor as direct effects. γ01 is the cross-level direct effect of W; it explains part of τ00. Slopes are still fixed (assumed equal across groups).

Step 3 — Random-Intercept Random-Slope (RIRSM): is τ11 ≠ 0?

Yij = γ00 + γ10(Xij−X̄j) + γ01(Wj−W̄) + u0j + u1j(Xij−X̄j) + rij

Now slopes vary too (variance τ11, plus the intercept-slope covariance τ01). Before hunting a moderator, test whether slopes really vary. Use these three tools — not a naive Wald CI:

1. Nonparametric residual bootstrap CI (Carpenter, Goldstein & Rasbash, 2003). Resample residuals (e.g., 1,500 reps); if the 95% CI for τ11 excludes 0, slopes vary. In the example: CI = [.004, .046].
2. −2 log-likelihood (deviance) test comparing Step 3 vs Step 2 (FIML). In the example: 1477.6 − 1469.5 = 8.1, p<.05.
3. Crainiceanu–Ruppert / RLRsim mixture-of-chi-squares simulation — superior Type-I control. In the example: p = .0013.
Do not build a Wald CI (estimate ±1.96×SE) around a variance component. Standard errors for variances are unreliable, and variances are non-negative so the normal-symmetry assumption fails.
Underpowered τ11 test? These tests use degrees of freedom from the (usually small) number of groups, so they are often underpowered. The default position is: if τ11 is not different from 0, don't search for specific interactions. However, to balance Type I and Type II error, you may proceed to the interaction test even when the null of no slope variance is retained — but only with a strong theory-based rationale, and you must explicitly acknowledge that τ11 was not significant so others can attempt replication.

Step 4 — Cross-Level Interaction model

Yij = γ00 + γ01(Wj−W̄) + γ10(Xij−X̄j) + γ11(Xij−X̄j)(Wj−W̄) + u0j + u1j(Xij−X̄j) + rij

Adds the product term. γ11 is the cross-level interaction effect.

Retain X even if its direct effect was non-significant. Standard interaction practice is to keep the lower-level term. A non-significant γ10 at Step 2 can become non-zero once W is added and centered; leaving X out forces γ10=0 at the centering point of W, distorting the model. So keep X in Step 4 regardless of its earlier significance.

Assumptions checklist

Multilevel modeling carries OLS-like assumptions. Violations risk model misspecification and grossly wrong standard errors. Tick each as you check it (saved with your inputs).

Phase 2–3 · Conduct & Analyze Recommendation 4

Centering / rescaling decision helper

Do: in most cross-level interaction tests, group-mean center the L1 predictor. Why: it yields the "unconflated" within-group estimate and minimizes spurious interactions. But the choice must follow your theory — record a justification.

Phase 2–3 · Conduct & Analyze Recommendation 5

Graphing the cross-level interaction

Do: plot simple slopes of Y on X at low / mean / high values of W to show the interaction's nature and direction. Why: a graph reveals the shape of moderation — but it must never be used to judge how large or important the effect is.

Coefficients & ranges for the plot

Y-axis range:

Three lines: simple slope of Y on X at W = −1 SD, mean, and +1 SD. Slope at a given W = γ10 + γ11·W; intercept = γ00 + γ01·W.

Regions of significance

Beyond plotting at ±1 SD, you can identify the region of significance — the range of W values over which the simple slope of Y on X is statistically significant. The article points to the Preacher, Curran & Bauer (2006) online tools (SAS / SPSS / R) for computing and plotting both simple slopes and regions of significance.

  Phase 4 · Interpret Recommendation 6
  
Symmetry of interpretation

  
Do: choose which variable is "the moderator" on conceptual grounds — usually the L2 contextual variable. Why: γ11 is mathematically identical whether you say W moderates X→Y or X moderates W→Y. The number doesn't change; only the framing does.


  

    
Because (X−X̄j)(W−W̄) = (W−W̄)(X−X̄j), the value of γ11 is the same either way. Convention in multilevel modeling is to treat the higher-level variable as the moderator, because it is conceptually natural to view the context as shaping a lower-level relationship.

  Phase 4 · Interpret Recommendation 7
  
Multiple cross-level interactions

  
Do: when testing more than one cross-level interaction, put them all in one model so each is adjusted for the others. Why: separate models can upwardly bias each effect through their intercorrelations.


  

    
Testing in one combined model is like running a proper omnibus test before follow-ups. Testing each interaction in its own model is like running follow-up comparisons with no omnibus — acceptable only with a strong theory-based rationale, and it inflates Type I error.

    
Valid reasons to test separately (must be reported transparently)

    
Strong theory-based rationale · the combined model will not converge · the model crashes · it runs out of degrees of freedom. If you test separately, state clearly (a) that you did, (b) why, and (c) the consequence — possible Type I error inflation requiring replication.

  Phase 4 · Interpret Recommendation 8
  
Three-or-more-level interactions

  
Do: extend the same two-way logic to three levels (e.g., individuals in teams in organizations, or repeated measures over time). Why: the procedure generalizes — but data demands grow steeply and no validated power tool exists for three-way cross-level interactions.


  

    
A three-level model (individuals i in teams j in organizations k) adds an L3 residual (ν0k) and L3 variance components: intercept variance φ00, slope variance φ11, intercept-slope covariance φ01. The full model expands the two-level equation to include all first-order effects, all two-way cross-level interactions, and finally the three-way term (X−X̄)(W−W̄)(Q−Q̄).

Power & data demands. Capturing variation in L1/L2 intercepts and slopes across L3 units requires many higher-level units. Studies often abandon three-level interactions because there is too little school-to-school (etc.) variation. Recommendation: there is no validated three-way power tool, so use the two-level Mathieu et al. (2012) calculator on each component to get some evidence about detectability, and require a strong theory-based rationale for positing a three-way effect.







  Phase 4 · Interpret Recommendation 9
  
Practical significance / effect size

  
Do: report effect size as both predictive power (γ11 in the original metric) and explanatory power (variance-explained indices), and place them in substantive context. Why: each captures something different and each has limits; a graph must never be used to judge size.


  

    
Option 1 — Predictive power: γ11 in the original metric

    
Directly interpretable in your scale's units, but scale-dependent — a 100-point outcome yields a much bigger γ11 than a 7-point one. Use caution comparing across studies with different scales.

    

  


  

    
Option 2 — Explanatory power: slope variance explained by W

    
Scale-independent. Proportion of across-group slope variance explained by the moderator: (τ11 − τ11w) / τ11, where τ11 is slope variance in the random-slope model (Step 3) and τ11w is the slope variance left unexplained after adding W (Step 4).

    

      

      

      

    

  


  

    
Option 3 — Pseudo R²

    
A familiar variance-explained index computed per step from the fixed part of the model only. Because it ignores random terms, it can change very little (or even decrease / go negative) when random coefficients are added — "now and then leads to unpleasant surprises" (Snijders & Bosker, 2012). Report it, but interpret carefully. See Module 10's table for per-step values.

    
Recommendation: report all three with statements about how each should be interpreted (full-disclosure / customer-centric reporting). References: Hox (2010); Roberts, Monaco, Stovall & Foster (2011); Snijders & Bosker (2012).

  


  
Context over size. A small proportion of variance explained does not automatically mean the effect is unimportant for theory or practice. Interpret every effect size within its substantive context — and never infer importance from how steep the lines look on a graph.

About & sources

  

    
What this is

    
An offline, single-file teaching workbench that operationalizes the ten best-practice recommendations of Aguinis, Gottfredson & Culpepper (2013) for estimating cross-level interaction effects with multilevel modeling. It guides you through Design → Conduct → Analyze → Interpret → Reporting using a worked example (LMX → empowerment, moderated by team leadership climate; 630 individuals in 105 teams) that you can replace with your own variables.

    
All statistical claims are drawn from the source article; this tool invents no thresholds or procedures and preserves the article's nuances (e.g., proceeding past weak slope-variance evidence only with strong theory). It is an independent aid and does not reproduce the article's text.
