ANCOVA with Fallible Covariates — a practical EIV toolkit

When you control for a covariate that is measured with error in a nonexperimental study, ordinary ANCOVA can manufacture treatment effects that are not real. This tool diagnoses your risk and applies the errors‑in‑variables (EIV) correction recommended by Culpepper & Aguinis (2011).

1 · What is the problem, and are you at risk?

A two‑minute orientation, then a guided checklist that tells you whether your study is exposed.

Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) adjusts a treatment effect for a covariate (for example, comparing two programs while "controlling for" prior ability). A built‑in assumption is that the covariate is measured without error. In real research that assumption is almost always false — questionnaires, tests, and ratings are fallibleFallible = measured with error. Its reliability ρxx is below 1..

Measurement error in the covariate biases the treatment effect only when both of these are true:

  • the covariate's reliability is below 1 (ρxxρxx — reliability of the covariate, between 0 and 1. 1 = perfectly reliable. < 1), and
  • the groups differ in their average covariate value (μ1x − μ0xDifference in the covariate's mean between the treatment and control groups, in standard‑deviation units. Zero in a perfectly randomized experiment. ≠ 0), which is the norm in nonexperimental designs.

If the groups are equivalent on the covariate, or the covariate is perfectly reliable, there is no bias. Otherwise, testing the treatment effect with ordinary OLS ANCOVA inflates the Type I error rateThe chance of declaring a treatment effect when none exists (a false positive). The nominal target is usually 0.05. — sometimes severely — so you may conclude a treatment works when it does not. The fix is the errors‑in‑variables (EIV) correction, which outperformed OLS, Lord's method, the Raaijmakers & Pieters method, and SEM across two Monte Carlo studies.

    5 · Which correction method should you use?

    A decision guide based on the article's two Monte Carlo studies.

    Default recommendation: use EIV. Across both studies it produced the most accurate treatment‑effect estimates, the highest statistical power, and Type I error rates closest to the nominal level. It handles one or several fallible covariates.

    How the methods compare

    MethodAvg. Type I error*Avg. power*Notes
    EIV (recommended).048.67Most accurate & powerful; relative bias ≈ 1.8–2.1% across ρxx .6–.9; handles multiple covariates/indicators.
    Lord (1960).007.28Limited to a single fallible covariate in pre/post designs. Overly conservative — deflated Type I error and low power.
    SEM (Sörbom 1978).033.612Handles multiple indicators but underestimated the treatment effect (~30% downward bias with multiple indicators in Study 2); bias 3.5–15.8% across ρxx.
    R&P (functional)——Least accurate of the methods evaluated; its equal‑variance assumption is violated whenever covariate reliability ≠ outcome variance explained.
    Naive OLS ANCOVAinflated—Ignores the problem; inflates Type I error when ρxx < 1 and groups differ. Not recommended.

    *Averages across simulated conditions, against a nominal α of .05. Source: Culpepper & Aguinis (2011).

    SEM and EIV both accept multiple indicators, but when you have a single measure of each construct and an estimate of its reliability, EIV is simpler and was the most accurate. Choose SEM only if you have a specific multi‑indicator measurement model you trust and reliability estimates are poor.
