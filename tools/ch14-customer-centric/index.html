Overview

Three steps from a significant result to publication-ready language

This tool operationalizes the customer-centric reporting approach of Aguinis et al. (2010). The “customers” of your research are both academics and practitioners. Reporting a result well means answering three questions in sequence — each builds on the one before it.

Step 1 · Is it likely real?

Statistical significance. Set a justified αAlpha (α) — a priori Type I error rate. The probability of rejecting a true null hypothesis (claiming an effect that isn’t there) that you are willing to accept. The article argues this should be reasoned from context, not fixed at .05. using the relative seriousness of Type I vs. Type II errors, and report the precise p value.

Step 2 · How big is it?

Effect magnitude. Report a standardized estimate of the size of the effect or relationship, with a confidence interval — and interpret it in context rather than against rigid small/medium/large cutoffs.

Step 3 · Does it matter?

Practical significance. Effect size is not practical significance. Plan a qualitative study so practitioners can say, in their own words, whether and why the result matters.

Why the order matters Steps 1 and 2 are quantitative and are logically necessary before Step 3. You first need to know the result is unlikely to be chance alone (Step 1) and how large it is (Step 2) before you can ask what it means in practice (Step 3).
Step 1 — Statistical significance

Set a justified α and report the precise p

Instead of defaulting to α = .05, derive an a priori Type I error rate that reflects how serious a false positive is relative to a false negative in your specific research context.

How context drives DRS — two contrasting cases from the article

Type II error more serious

CEO earnings-manipulation incentives (Zhang et al., 2008). Concluding there is no link between certain incentives and earnings manipulation when there really is one could let firms adopt those incentives — risking stock-price collapse, reputational damage, turnover, even bankruptcy. The cost of a miss (Type II) is severe, so you would tolerate a higher α (DRS < 1).

Type I error more serious

Interdivisional knowledge transfer & invention impact (Miller, Fern & Cardinal, 2007). Concluding there is a relationship when there isn’t would push firms to pour money, time and resources into cross-division knowledge sharing for no gain. The false positive is costlier, so you would keep α lower (DRS > 1).

The point: the “right” α depends on what each error would cost in your setting. Reason it through and write it down.

Report the precise p value

Enter your observed p value to generate reporting language and compare it to the α you justified above.

Avoid “crippled” p values Reporting only p < .05 dichotomizes a continuous 0–1 scale and throws away information — p = .000001 and p = .049 would look identical. Report the actual value (e.g. p = .04) to 2–3 decimals and let readers judge against their own α. Do not lean on arbitrary conventional cutoffs.
Step 2 — Effect magnitude

Report the size of the effect, with a confidence interval

Statistical significance says nothing about how strong the effect is. Compute a standardized effect-magnitude estimate (Table 3 of the article) and report a confidence intervalConfidence interval. A range that, across repeated samples, would contain the true effect a stated percentage of the time (here 95%). It conveys the precision of your estimate — a wide interval signals uncertainty that a single point estimate hides. around it.

Group differences — Cohen’s d & Hedges’s g

Use when the null hypothesis is about a relationship among variables; interpreted as the proportion of variance in the outcome explained by the predictors.

Adjusted R² = 1 − (1 − R²)(N−1)/(N−k−1). It corrects for capitalization on chance when N is small and k is large. The 95% CI uses the Olkin–Finn large-sample approximation.

Do not use one-size-fits-all small / medium / large cutoffs Cohen’s .10/.30/.50 benchmarks were set subjectively — partly from values observed in a single 1960 psychology journal volume, partly from Cohen’s own opinion (he later lowered them and acknowledged they “were made subjectively”). Applying them rigidly, Thompson warned, is “being stupid in a new metric.” Interpret magnitude in the context of your phenomenon, prior effects in the area, and what the outcome means — not against a fixed table.

Factors that shrink or inflate an estimate — caveat accordingly

FactorEffect on the estimate
Measurement reliabilityLower reliability → smaller estimates
Number of scale anchorsFewer anchors (coarser scale) → smaller estimates
Sample heterogeneity / varianceLess heterogeneity & variance → smaller estimates
Sample size & number of predictorsSmall N with many predictors → inflated (capitalization on chance) → correct it
Research designNonexperimental designs → usually smaller than experimental

Always state which specific estimate you computed, whether it is adjusted, and its confidence interval.

Step 3 — Practical significance

Plan a qualitative study: does it matter, to whom, in what context?

Effect size ≠ practical significance. To establish whether a result actually matters, gather evidence in practitioners’ own discourse through a qualitative study. This planner helps you design it.

A common confusion to avoid An effect-magnitude estimate is not a study’s practical significance. Knowing a test correlates with performance at r = .30 (9% of variance) does not tell a practitioner whether to adopt it, how much better hiring would be, or what the unexplained 91% costs. Practical significance is a value judgment made by the consumer of the research, in context.

Keep expectations calibrated This step is most valuable for new areas of research, and it is not mandatory for every study. One qualitative study cannot prove the universal worth of a result — establishing the external validity of practical significance may require more than one qualitative study, with replicability and transparency throughout.
