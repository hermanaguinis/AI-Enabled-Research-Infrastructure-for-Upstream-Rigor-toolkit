2C — Common Errors to Avoid

Error 1: Vague reporting. Don't write "we ran regression diagnostics and removed outliers." Name every technique used, the cutoff value applied, how many cases were flagged, and how each was classified. Transparency is a core principle in Aguinis (2025).
Error 2: Incongruent technique. The identification technique must match the outlier definition. For example: if you define outliers as influential on prediction, you must use DFFITSᵢ, Cook's Dᵢ, or DFBETASᵢⱼ — not SD analysis (which measures single-construct deviation, not prediction influence).
Error 3: Treating all outliers as problems. Not every extreme score is an error. Flagged cases may be interesting outliers containing substantive knowledge worth studying, or they may be legitimate data points with negligible influence on results. Investigate before acting.

Panel A — Error Outliers

✔ Correct handling — do BOTH of the following:

Step 1: Correct or Remove

If possible, correct the value to its true value (e.g., by contacting the respondent, if IRB permits). If correction is not possible, remove the case from the dataset. Do not simply flag it and move on.

Step 2: Document in Detail

Report the nature of the error in your manuscript. Was it a coding error? A data entry error? A case outside the target population? An impossible value (e.g., age = 200)? A misunderstood survey question?

✘ Incorrect handling to avoid: Automatically deleting any deviant data point without first verifying it was actually caused by a data error. Deletion without verification conflates error outliers with interesting or influential outliers.
⚠ IRB note: If your IRB protocol requires data anonymization, keep detailed diaries or logs during data collection to enable retrospective investigation of unusual cases after data collection is complete. This makes it possible to verify whether flagged cases are genuine errors.

Panel B — Interesting Outliers

✔ Correct handling: Study the outlier. Do not simply remove it.

Option 1: Quantitative Study

Compare the interesting outlier(s) to non-outliers on theoretically relevant characteristics. Use statistical tests to identify what distinguishes the extreme cases from the rest of the sample. This approach is most useful when you have several interesting outliers.

Option 2: Qualitative Study

Apply a case study method to identify the unique attributes of extreme cases. For example, select the top and bottom cases in a distribution and examine their organizational, contextual, or behavioral features in depth. Both options can be used together.

A priori vs. post hoc interesting outliers: An a priori interesting outlier is defined before data collection as a theoretically extreme case of interest. A post hoc interesting outlier is identified during or after data analysis as an unexpected extreme case. Both are valuable, but post hoc classification should be clearly labeled as exploratory.
✘ Incorrect handling to avoid:
(1) Treating interesting outliers as errors and deleting them without justification.
(2) Investigating whether they affect results (i.e., treating them as influential outliers) without studying what makes them substantively interesting.

Three Handling Options (All Contexts)

Option 1: Respecify the Model

Add terms to the model that may account for the outlier's extreme behavior — nonlinear terms, interaction terms (moderators), or cross-level interactions (MLM). If the added term is theoretically supported, report it in the Future Research section as a potential avenue — not as a confirmed finding. Respecification capitalizes on chance and should not be used as a primary theory-testing strategy.

Option 2: Delete the Outlier

Remove the case from the analysis. You MUST report results both with and without the deletion. If results differ, you must explain why and discuss the implications. Deletion without disclosure suggests you selected the result that favors your hypotheses.

Option 3: Robust Approaches

Use estimation methods that reduce the weight of extreme cases rather than deleting them. See options below. Always report results from both the standard and the robust approach.

Least Absolute Deviation (LAD)

Minimizes the sum of absolute (not squared) residuals; less sensitive to extreme values.

Least Trimmed Squares (LTS)

Removes the cases with the highest squared residuals before estimation.

M-Estimation

Replaces the squared residuals in the objective function with a less rapidly increasing function, reducing outlier influence.

Bayesian Statistics

Prior information shrinks extreme observations toward the center of the distribution.

Two-Stage Robust (SEM/Reg)

Computes Mahalanobis distance to identify extreme predictor cases and downweights them in estimation.

Iteratively Reweighted Least Squares (SEM)

Similar to two-stage robust but uses an iterative IRLS algorithm to achieve robustness.

GEE Methods (MLM)

Estimates variances and covariances from residuals with weaker distributional assumptions. Approximates random effects only.

Bootstrapping (MLM)

Does not assume a theoretical sampling distribution. Requires n > 150 for stable estimates.

⚡ Transparency Requirement — Always report results BOTH ways

Always present results with and without the handling technique. Provide a clear explanation for any differences in findings. This is the core principle of transparent outlier reporting (Aguinis, 2025) and prevents readers from suspecting that outlier treatment was selected to support the hypotheses.
