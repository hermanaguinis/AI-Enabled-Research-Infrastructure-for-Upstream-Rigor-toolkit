What Is Theory Elaboration?

Theory elaboration is a third path for researchers — distinct from generating brand-new theory or simply testing existing hypotheses. This tab explains what it is, how it differs from related approaches, and why it matters for organizational research.

Formal Definition:
"Theory elaboration is the process of conceptualizing and executing empirical research using preexisting conceptual ideas or a preliminary model as a basis for developing new theoretical insights by contrasting, specifying, or structuring theoretical constructs and relations to account for and explain empirical observations."
— Fisher & Aguinis (2017, p. 441), as extended in Aguinis (2025)

The Four Constituent Elements

In plain language, theory elaboration has four defining features:

1
Both conceptual AND empirical: Theory elaboration is not a purely conceptual exercise (like an AMR theory paper) nor purely a data analysis. It requires both specifying constructs/relations conceptually AND assessing them empirically.
2
Starts with an existing theory: You do not begin from scratch. You use an existing theory or preliminary model as your starting point — even if that theory is incomplete or inadequate.
3
Goal = accurate empirical explanation: The purpose is to refine the existing theory so it more accurately explains what is actually observed in real organizational settings.
4
Core tactics = contrasting, specifying, or structuring: You improve the theory by contrasting it across contexts, specifying its constructs more precisely, or structuring its relationships more accurately.

Process & Tactics Induct constructs and relationships from data; develop new concepts using logical argument
Plain language: You let the data speak — looking for patterns and building new concepts from the ground up. This is the classic grounded theory approach.

Example: Navis & Ozbek (2016) used inductive reasoning to develop a new framework explaining how identity and cognition combine to produce the paradox of entrepreneurial entry.
 Collect and analyze data to assess whether they provide evidence supporting hypothesized relationships
Plain language: You form your hypotheses first, then collect data specifically to test them. The theory drives your data collection and analysis.

Example: A researcher surveys 300 employees to test whether transformational leadership predicts team performance, using confirmatory factor analysis and structural equation modeling.
 Use existing concepts and models to collect and organize data; contrast, specify, and structure theoretical constructs and relations to refine existing theory
Plain language: You use both inductive and deductive reasoning simultaneously — the existing theory guides your data collection, and the data guides you to elaborate the theory.

Example: Shane (2000) used prior opportunity-recognition theory to guide data collection across the eight teams, then used the data to elaborate which factors (prior knowledge domains) explain why the same opportunity was perceived and exploited so differently.
Output New testable propositions; new constructs
Plain language: You end up with a brand-new theory or framework that didn't exist before — with new concepts and new hypotheses that others can test.

Example: Navis & Ozbek (2016) produced new theoretical propositions about how identity-based reasoning leads entrepreneurs to enter markets despite poor odds.
 Accept or reject hypotheses derived from extant theory
Plain language: You either confirm or disconfirm existing theoretical predictions. The theory itself is not changed — you are evaluating it.

Example: The study finds support for H1 (transformational leadership → team performance, β=.34, p<.01) and concludes that the theory is supported in this context.
 Refinement of existing theoretical ideas — refined contextual factors, constructs, and/or relationships
Plain language: You end up with an improved version of the existing theory — one that is more precise, more accurate, or more complete than it was before.

Example: Shane (2000) elaborated opportunity-recognition theory by specifying that prior knowledge in three domains (customer problems, ways to serve markets, technology) predicts which aspects of an opportunity an entrepreneur will discover.

How Theory Elaboration Differs from Related Concepts

Theory elaboration vs. grounded theory: Grounded theory is one method that can be used to conduct a theory elaboration study — but the two are not the same. Grounded theory generates theory from data with no prior theoretical framework. Theory elaboration specifically starts with an existing theory and uses it to guide data collection and elaboration. A theory elaboration study may use grounded theory methods, but it will always include an explicit prior theoretical framework as a starting point.

Theory elaboration vs. abductive reasoning: Abductive reasoning (inference to the best explanation) is philosophically related — both involve moving between theory and data iteratively. However, theory elaboration provides more specific, actionable guidelines. It names concrete tactics (horizontal/vertical contrasting, construct specification, structuring relations) and connects each to a specific theory advancement goal (improving construct validity, logical adequacy, explanatory adequacy, etc.).

Important Nuance
Most real studies include elements of all three modes (generation, testing, elaboration). Theory elaboration is not a rigid category — it is a primary focus and orientation. What distinguishes a theory elaboration study is that its primary contribution is the refinement of an existing theory through both conceptual specification and empirical assessment. The researcher neither generates theory from scratch nor merely tests existing hypotheses — they start with an existing model and use it inductively to elaborate.

Should I Use Theory Elaboration?

Use this three-question decision guide to determine whether theory elaboration is the right approach for your research. Click Yes or No at each step. Based on Fisher & Aguinis (2017) Figure 1 and Aguinis (2025).

Question 1 of 3

Question 1 of 3

Is there an existing theory or preliminary model that may explain or offer insight into your focal phenomenon, AND do you have access to data that can be used to evaluate that phenomenon?

What this means
Theory elaboration requires two prerequisites: (1) an existing theoretical foundation to build on, and (2) access to empirical data. If either is missing, elaboration is not yet possible.

❌ Theory Elaboration Is Not Appropriate Here

Consider deductive theory development (e.g., an AMR-style conceptual paper): build a new theoretical framework from scratch using logical argumentation, without the need for an existing theory to elaborate on.

Why: Theory elaboration requires a starting theoretical base. Without an existing theory or preliminary model, there is nothing to elaborate — and without data access, the empirical grounding that defines theory elaboration is impossible.

Question 2 of 3

Is the explanation provided by the existing theory controversial, ambiguous, or inadequate in important ways — meaning there are mixed results, unanswered questions, underspecified constructs, or unexplained phenomena?

What this means
Theory elaboration adds the most value when existing theory is not sufficient. If the theory already explains the phenomenon fully and accurately, there may be no need to elaborate — testing it may be enough.

❌ Consider Theory Testing Instead

The existing theory appears adequate for your purposes. Develop formal hypotheses deductively from the existing theory and test them empirically using a theory testing design.

Why: Theory elaboration adds the most value when existing theory is insufficient. If the theory is adequate, the elaboration approach is unnecessary — and reviewers may question why you did not simply test existing predictions.

Question 3 of 3

Is there potential to collect additional empirical data that, when integrated with existing theory, could help develop, enhance, or extend existing theoretical insights — allowing for both inductive and deductive theorizing?

What this means
Theory elaboration has a dual empirical-conceptual focus. You need data not just to test hypotheses, but to engage in the back-and-forth between theory and data that is the hallmark of elaboration. Can you collect data in a way that will let you both test and discover?

❌ Consider Deductive Theory Development

Without the possibility of new data collection, a purely deductive theory development approach (conceptual paper) is more appropriate. Theory elaboration has a dual empirical-conceptual focus and requires data to contrast, specify, or structure theoretical relations.

✓ Theory Elaboration Is Right for Your Research

Theory elaboration is a viable and potentially highly beneficial approach. You have an existing theoretical foundation, the theory has meaningful limitations, and you can collect data to both inform and refine it.

Section 3B: The Three Approaches and Seven Tactics

Expand each approach panel to see the specific tactics, when to use them, and published examples.

■ Approach 1: Contrasting ▼

Plain-language introduction: Contrasting involves comparing how a theory applies in a different setting from the one it was originally developed in. It helps you discover what is truly generalizable about a theory and what varies by context or level of analysis.

Primary theory advancement purpose: Improving logical and empirical adequacy.

Implementation Guideline 1: "If the primary research focus is to improve logical and/or empirical validity of an existing theory, then a contrasting approach and tactics can be employed as part of a theory elaboration study." (Fisher & Aguinis, 2017)
Tactic 1A — Horizontal Contrasting Same Level, New Context

Examining how an existing theoretical insight fits in a context different from that for which it was developed, while keeping the level of analysis constant. The context varies (e.g., different industry, geographic setting, organizational type) but the level (e.g., individual, team, organization) remains the same.

Key output: Generalization of the theory to a new context; deeper understanding of which theoretical elements vary across contexts.

When to use: When a theory was developed in one specific organizational, industry, or geographic context and you want to examine whether it holds in a different context at the same level. Most useful in new context research opportunity situations (see Tab 4).

Published Example 1: Maguire, Hardy, & Lawrence (2004) compared institutional change initiated by central actors in a mature field (prior theory) with institutional change initiated by peripheral actors in an emerging field (their data context). Same level (organizational field), different context.

Published Example 2: Greenwood & Suddaby (2006) examined institutional entrepreneurship in the context of mature professional fields, contrasting with prior theory developed in other contexts.
Tactic 1B — Vertical Contrasting Same Constructs, New Level

Comparing a theory developed to explain constructs and relations at one level of analysis with data describing constructs and relations at a different level. Examples include adapting individual-level concepts (cognition, learning, identity) to explain organizational-level phenomena. The level of analysis changes while examining the same theoretical constructs.

When to use: When a theory developed at one level (e.g., individual cognition) could shed light on phenomena at another level (e.g., organizational behavior). Most useful in theory borrowing and cross-level effects situations (see Tab 4). Carefully assess which aspects of the theory function similarly across levels and which aspects change.

Published Example: Tripsas & Gavetti (2000) applied individual-level cognition theory to examine how top management teams' collective cognitive representations directed organizational search processes and influenced capability accumulation at Polaroid. Same theoretical constructs (cognition, mental models), different level (individual → organizational).
■ Approach 2: Construct Specification ▼

Plain-language introduction: Construct specification involves identifying and defining constructs more precisely so they accurately reflect empirical realities. You either discover a new construct that the existing theory missed, or recognize that a single broad construct actually comprises multiple distinct dimensions.

Primary theory advancement purpose: Improving construct validity and construct scope.

Implementation Guideline 2: "If the primary focus is to improve the validity and/or scope of a construct, then the construct specification approach and tactics can be employed as part of a theory elaboration study." (Fisher & Aguinis, 2017)
Tactic 2A — New Construct Specification Discover a Missing Construct

Identifying and defining a construct that has not previously been considered in existing theory. Through empirical work, a researcher recognizes a new theoretical unit that is necessary to accurately depict reality. The new construct often emerges from a grounded theory or inductive approach and creates opportunities to recognize new theoretical relations.

When to use: When you notice a concept in your empirical data that cannot be captured by existing constructs and that would significantly improve the theory's ability to explain the phenomenon. Most useful in emergent theory and new context situations (see Tab 4).

Published Example: Gioia & Chittipeddi (1991) identified the construct of sensegiving — "the process of attempting to influence the sensemaking and meaning construction of others toward a preferred redefinition of organizational reality" — as a necessary complement to the existing sensemaking construct. Without the application of existing sensemaking theory, the recognition of sensegiving might never have occurred. The new construct dramatically improved the theory's explanatory power.
Tactic 2B — Construct Splitting Divide a Broad Construct

Recognizing that a single existing construct actually comprises multiple distinct dimensions and partitioning it into two or more constructs. This improves discriminant validity, resolves contradictions in prior research, and provides more accurate theoretical linkages.

When to use: When prior research on a construct shows mixed or contradictory results and you suspect the inconsistency stems from the fact that the construct is actually multidimensional. When examining how a construct behaves in reality reveals multiple distinct forms or perspectives.

Published Example 1: Ely & Thomas (2001) split the broad construct of "workforce diversity" into three distinct perspectives — integration-and-learning, access-and-legitimacy, and discrimination-and-fairness — each with different implications for workgroup functioning. This resolved prior contradictions in the diversity literature that had produced mixed results.

Published Example 2: Maitlis (2005) identified four distinct forms of organizational sensemaking, splitting what had been treated as a single construct.
■ Approach 3: Structuring ▼

Plain-language introduction: Structuring involves elaborating on theoretical relations so they accurately describe and explain what is observed empirically. This approach either identifies relations not previously described or explains complex sequential or recursive interactions not fully captured in prior theory.

Primary theory advancement purpose: Improving explanatory and predictive adequacy.

Implementation Guideline 3: "If the primary focus is to improve the explanatory and predictive adequacy of an existing theory, then the structuring approach and tactics can be employed as part of a theory elaboration study." (Fisher & Aguinis, 2017)
Tactic 3A — Specific Relation Structures Clarify HOW & WHY

Identifying and describing specific relations between constructs that have not previously been described, or identifying the mechanisms that underlie known relations. This may involve moving from a direct linear relationship to moderation, mediation (through a new construct), or nonlinear relationships — specifying previously unknown connections or unpacking the mechanisms driving known relations.

When to use: When the mechanism connecting two constructs is poorly understood even though the relation itself is known; when you want to specify boundary conditions (moderators) or mediating processes; when prior theory identifies that X and Y are related but does not explain HOW or WHY.

Published Example 1: Greenwood & Suddaby (2006) elaborated institutional entrepreneurship theory by specifying WHICH central actors are more likely to institute change and isolating the MECHANISMS driving such change.

Published Example 2: Shane (2000) specified three knowledge domains (customer problems, ways to serve markets, and technology) that enable entrepreneurs to discover different aspects of opportunities.

Published Example 3: Weick (1993) identified four sources of resilience in organizations facing collapse.
Tactic 3B — Sequence Relation Structures Temporal Ordering of Events

Providing an explanation of a sequence of events or relations over time. Theory elaboration allows examination of temporal ordering and probabilistic interaction between variables — moving from variance theories ("more X produces more Y") to process theories ("do A, then B, to get C"). Uses existing theoretical constructs to organize data and gain new insights into temporal processes.

When to use: When organizational phenomena need to be understood by considering the temporal ordering of events; when the question is not just WHETHER something happens but WHEN and IN WHAT ORDER; when comparing successful and unsuccessful sequences can reveal critical process differences.

Published Example 1: Edmondson, Bohmer, & Pisano (2001) identified four sequential process steps underlying successful technology adoption in hospitals — comparing learning sequences in hospitals that successfully vs. unsuccessfully implemented new cardiac surgery technology.

Published Example 2: Gioia & Chittipeddi (1991) identified four phases of strategic change (envisioning → signaling → re-visioning → energizing), each with different sensemaking and sensegiving dynamics.
Tactic 3C — Recursive Relation Structures Feedback Loops Over Time

Accounting for and describing recursive interactions — what happens when two or more constructs interact repeatedly over time. Rather than a one-time relationship (X → Y), recursive structures capture bidirectional, feedback-driven relationships that evolve through repeated interactions (X ↔ Y). Two types: (1) dyadic interactions between actors or entities, and (2) multilevel interactions between constructs at different levels of analysis.

When to use: When a theory identifies a relationship between two constructs but the recursive, feedback nature of that relationship over time is poorly understood; when examining how a relationship develops and evolves through repeated interactions; particularly valuable in theory reversal situations (see Tab 4).

Published Example: Tripsas & Gavetti (2000) identified that managerial cognitive representations → direct organizational search → which influences capability accumulation → which in turn shapes managerial cognitive representations (loop back). This recursive relationship between cognitions, capabilities, and firm inertia explained Polaroid's failure to respond effectively to digital photography.

Limitations & Safeguards

Theory elaboration is a powerful but not unlimited approach. Being aware of its limitations and taking active steps to mitigate them will strengthen your study and your manuscript. Based on Fisher & Aguinis (2017) and Aguinis (2025).

⚠️ Limitation 1: Risk of Overly Complex Theory

The Limitation

Theory elaboration can result in theories that are too complex to be understood, remembered, or applied broadly. Researchers walk a fine line between adding needed nuance and making the theory unwieldy or inaccessible to practitioners and future researchers.

Why It Occurs

Each elaboration (new construct, new relation, new boundary condition) adds complexity. As researchers engage deeply with rich empirical data, the temptation is to capture every nuance — but theories that try to explain everything often explain nothing well.

Safeguard: Aim for parsimony alongside accuracy. After elaboration, evaluate whether each addition genuinely improves explanatory power independently. Cut constructs or relations that do not carry independent explanatory weight. Ask yourself: "Is this complexity truly necessary to account for the empirical observations I made, or is it adding detail for detail's sake?"

⚠️ Limitation 2: Risk of Limited Generalizability

The Limitation

Over-elaborating a theory to account for context-specific factors can produce a theory so tailored to one setting that it loses external validity. The more a theory is refined to fit one context, the less it may apply to others.

Why It Occurs

Theory elaboration typically involves deep engagement with specific empirical contexts — a single industry, a specific cultural setting, a particular time period. Context-specific findings, while rich, may not generalize.

Safeguard: Clearly delineate which aspects of your elaborated theory are context-specific and which are generalizable. Include explicit boundary conditions in your propositions. Discuss the scope conditions of your elaboration in the discussion section. Collect data from multiple contexts if possible to build confidence in generalizability.

⚠️ Limitation 3: Dependence on Starting Theory Quality

The Limitation

If the underlying theory you choose to elaborate is weak or flawed, the elaboration may perpetuate and amplify those weaknesses. A theory elaboration study is only as strong as the theoretical foundation it builds on.

Why It Occurs

Theory elaboration starts from an existing model and uses it to guide data collection. If the model has fundamental problems (poor construct validity, logical inconsistencies, lack of empirical support), those problems will be imported into the elaboration.

Safeguard: Before beginning, critically and thoroughly assess the strength of the theory you are using as a foundation. Review meta-analyses and systematic reviews of the theory's empirical support. If the theory has fundamental logical or empirical problems, address those explicitly in your paper, or consider whether theory building (starting fresh) is more appropriate for your research question.

⚠️ Limitation 4: Perspective Dependence

The Limitation

Any theoretical advancement from theory elaboration is inherently bounded by the perspective and assumptions of the starting theory. You can elaborate a theory, but you cannot fundamentally escape its core assumptions, ontology, or worldview while remaining within theory elaboration.

Why It Occurs

Theory elaboration works within an existing theoretical tradition — it refines and extends, rather than replaces. Alternative theoretical perspectives that start from different assumptions may produce very different insights from the same empirical observations.

Safeguard: Acknowledge explicitly which theoretical perspective your elaboration is grounded in and what alternative perspectives might yield different insights. Discuss these alternatives in your limitations section. If the goal of your research is to fundamentally challenge a theoretical tradition rather than to refine it, theory building (or theory replacement) may be more appropriate.

⚠️ Limitation 5: Subjectivity Concerns

The Limitation

Because theory elaboration often includes qualitative elements and an iterative back-and-forth between theory and data, conclusions are not always based on formal statistical tests. Some readers and reviewers may perceive qualitative insights as less rigorous than quantitative hypothesis testing.

Why It Occurs

The inductive-deductive interplay that defines theory elaboration means that the researcher's judgment plays a significant role in identifying patterns, connecting observations to theory, and choosing which refinements to make. This is inherently more subjective than running a regression.

Safeguard: Be explicit about your reasoning and evidence at every step of the elaboration process. Connect each elaborated theoretical insight tightly to specific, concrete empirical observations (with direct quotes, data excerpts, or figures where possible). Use multiple data sources and types to triangulate. Describe your analytic process transparently — including how you moved between theory and data — so readers can evaluate the basis for your theoretical claims.

✅ Pre-Submission Checklist

Verify each item before submitting your theory elaboration manuscript:

  • I have explicitly stated that I am adopting a theory elaboration approach (rather than labeling the study as theory generation or theory testing)
  • I have clearly described the existing theory or model I used as a starting point
  • I have justified why the existing theory was insufficient — specifically, how it is controversial, ambiguous, or inadequate
  • I have explained how my data collection allowed for both inductive and deductive theorizing simultaneously
  • I have named the specific tactic(s) I used: horizontal contrasting, vertical contrasting, new construct specification, construct splitting, specific relation structures, sequence relation structures, and/or recursive relation structures
  • I have connected each empirical observation explicitly to a refinement of the theoretical model — showing not just what I found but how each finding changes the theory
  • I have articulated what the elaborated theory adds that the original theory could not explain
  • I have addressed generalizability and boundary conditions — specifying what is context-specific and what is broadly applicable
  • I have cited Aguinis (2025) and Fisher & Aguinis (2017) as the methodological framework for my theory elaboration approach
