What is a mixed methods design?

A mixed methods design includes at least one quantitative method (examining numbers) and at least one qualitative method (examining text). The core premise is that combining the two yields a better understanding of complex phenomena than either approach could provide alone — for example, by triangulating one set of results with another to strengthen the validity of inferences.

Designing a study comes down to a few decisions this tool walks you through: why you are mixing (purpose), which strand leads (priority), and when each happens (sequence). Those answers produce a named design and a standard notation you can drop into a proposal.

The five purposes for mixing

Greene, Caracelli & Graham (1989). You will select the ones that apply in the wizard.

Decision 1 — Priority / emphasis

Equal priority (equivalent-status design): both strands are used about equally to understand the phenomenon.

One method dominant (dominant–less-dominant / nested design): a lead method carries the study with a small component of the other — QUAN-dominant, QUAL-dominant, or equal.

Justify the choice using three things: your research question, practical data-collection constraints, and the presumed preference of your intended audience.

Decision 2 — Implementation / sequence

Concurrent (simultaneous): quantitative and qualitative data are gathered at the same time.

Sequential (in phases):

QUAL → QUAN: first explore the problem, then follow up with quantitative data on a large sample.

QUAN → QUAL: e.g., run a cluster/statistical analysis first, then do in-depth qualitative follow-up.

Notation

Express your design compactly. Capitalization signals priority (UPPERCASE = dominant strand, lowercase = less-dominant). The arrow vs. the plus signals sequence (→ sequential, + concurrent).

QUAL → quan QUAN → qual QUAN + QUAL

Integrate process and outcome

The quantitative strand typically estimates the statistical effects of antecedents on outcomes. The qualitative strand illuminates process — how phenomena emerge, and how and why variables influence outcomes. Strong designs deliberately connect the two so process and outcome insights inform one another.

    About this tool

    This planner operationalizes the mixed methods guidance in Aguinis & Molina-Azórín (2015), "Using multilevel modeling and mixed methods to make theoretical progress in microfoundations for strategy research," Strategic Organization, 13(4), 353–364. It covers the definition of mixed methods, the five purposes for mixing (Greene et al., 1989), the priority and sequence decisions, standard notation, the participant-selection design (Rouse & Daellenbach, 1999), and the integration of process and outcome insights.

    Key sources

    Greene, J. C., Caracelli, V. J., & Graham, W. F. (1989). Toward a conceptual framework for mixed-method evaluation designs. Educational Evaluation and Policy Analysis.
    Rouse, M. J., & Daellenbach, U. S. (1999). Rethinking research methods for the resource-based perspective. Strategic Management Journal.
    Aguinis, H., & Molina-Azórín, J. F. (2015). Strategic Organization, 13(4), 353–364.

    No. The tool is a single HTML file with no external connections. Your entries are stored only in this browser (localStorage) and included in exports you generate yourself.
