Welcome

Design a rigorous, valid thought experiment

A thought experiment is a judgment about what would happen if an imagined scenario were real. It is conducted not in a physical lab but in “the laboratory of the mind.” This guided tool walks you through every step of planning, executing, reporting, and discussing a thought experiment so you finish with a fully specified design and an exportable write-up ready to drop into a manuscript.

“Thought experiments illustrate one way that theorizing can bridge the gap between the abstract and the concrete.” — Folger & Turillo (1999)

Why use a thought experiment?

💰
Cost-efficient
Conducted entirely in the researcher’s mind — no recruiting, compensation, equipment, or sample-size constraints. They can be run for little to no financial cost.
✅
No IRB approval needed
Because the scenario is imagined, it is not subject to the same ethical and legal review as traditional experiments — ideal for socially sensitive topics (discrimination, harassment, bullying).
♾
Unlimited variables
You can manipulate a virtually unlimited number of imagined independent variables, free of fatigue, acquiescence, or logistical limits.
🔬
Advances theory
Used for decades across physics, economics, philosophy and more to confirm or disconfirm theory — yet they remain underutilized in OB and adjacent fields.

What this tool will produce

Working through the eight modules, you will (1) decide whether a thought experiment is appropriate, (2) identify which of four types fits your situation, (3) plan the theory, (4) design the execution, (5) plan how to report results, and (6) frame the implications. At the end you receive a structured draft (copyable and downloadable as Markdown/text) plus a completeness checklist. All of your work is held in memory in your browser only — nothing is uploaded or saved to a server.

Module 2 · Which type?

Taxonomy of Thought Experiments

Thought experiments are classified along two dimensions: the theory’s development stage (early vs. late) and the study’s theoretical goal (confirmation vs. disconfirmation). This 2×2 yields four types. The cell recommended by your Module 1 answers is highlighted — but you may override it by clicking a different cell.

TYPE I
Early · Confirmation
Building a nascent theory and seeking to confirm rather than disconfirm it.
Exemplar: Newton’s cannonball — imagined cannonballs fired ever faster until one orbits the Earth, confirming nascent ideas about gravity.
TYPE II
Early · Disconfirmation
A counterfactual or paradoxical situation that questions an early-stage theory and forces refinement.
Exemplar: Schrödinger’s cat — a paradox (a cat simultaneously alive and dead) that disconfirms an early interpretation of quantum mechanics.
Late
stage
TYPE III
Late · Confirmation
Confirming a more developed theory.
Exemplar: Galileo and the Leaning Tower of Pisa — imagining two bodies of different weight falling in equal time, confirming his developed theory of gravity.
TYPE IV
Late · Disconfirmation
A paradoxical situation that disconfirms a developed theory.
Exemplar: Einstein riding a beam of light — a paradox under the prevailing emission theory of light, which it disconfirmed.
Module 3 · Plan it

Theory Considerations

Planning means building an engaging, convincing “mind model” — understanding and relaying the theory being tested, including its propositions, dimensions, and assumptions. Work through each prompt below.

Review the key constructs, variables, and relevant background that create the “mind model” and set the scene for your experiment.

Example (allyship): Allyship = members of advantaged groups taking committed action to improve the treatment and status of a disadvantaged group. Key constructs: effective vs. performative allyship; self-determination; supportive contact.

Name the one proposition or assumption you will challenge or affirm. Anchor it in a debate or a commonly misunderstood relationship.

Example: Theoretical tension over who determines the value of allyship — the well-intentioned ally, or the intended beneficiary?

List the assumptions that set the boundaries of the experiment. Making them explicit defines the conditions under which your conclusion holds.

Example: Assume the ally has prior experience supporting BIPOC colleagues; the beneficiary has already acted on her own behalf; she has substantial knowledge of the organization’s systems.

Sketch out what visual(s) will illustrate the dimensions of the theory in your mind model.

Example: A figure mapping the dominant-group member’s options — use own judgment / consult & collaborate / enact the beneficiary’s request — against self-determination and mentoring theory.
Module 4 · Execute it

Research Design Considerations

Execution means conducting the experiment in the laboratory of the mind using the model you developed when planning. Above all, be a storyteller and apply “disciplined imagination.”

Write your experiment as a narrative, storylike scenario that bridges the abstract and the concrete. This is the heart of your thought experiment.

Example: Dr. Beauford Manigold, a White male department chair, is asked to meet Dr. Dianne Winfrey, a junior Black female colleague he helped recruit. She asks him to lobby to renegotiate her tenure benchmarks and shield her from DEI service. He must choose how to respond...

Ensure consistent internal and external logic, then state why the boundaries you proposed make sense.

Example: Even the most fantastical thought experiment (Einstein on a light beam) is grounded in natural, explainable phenomena. State why your rejected assumptions are reasonable given the scenario.

Will you use more than one contrasting scenario to test the theory? If so, describe them (optional).

Tie the abstract theory to an understandable situation drawn from the present, the distant past, or an imagined future.

Example: “What if Dr. Manigold simply enacts the request as stated?” vs. “What if he instead consults, mentors, and advocates?” — contrasting outcomes for self-determination.

Module 6 · Discuss implications

Discussing Implications

Implications begin with the shift in thinking the experiment produces, then surface hidden truths and connect to other disciplines.

What shift in thinking or perspective results from your experiment?

Example: The field should shift focus from self-identified allies of the dominant group to the role of the intended beneficiaries in determining allyship’s value.

What obscure truths or biases did the experiment illuminate? What alternative explanations exist?

Example: The experiment clarifies the line between effective and performative allyship, and surfaces the value of exchanging organizational knowledge across vantage points.

How do your implications link to or add value for other disciplines?

Example: Draws together self-determination theory and mentoring theory; relevant to social psychology, education, and organizational justice.
