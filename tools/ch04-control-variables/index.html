Why this matters

Adding controls is not automatically “safer.” Each pitfall below is what this tool helps you avoid.

The “purification principle” is an assumption, not a fact

The belief that controls remove contamination and reveal “true” relationships rests on unproven assumptions: that the control distorts the focal relationship, that it is measured reliably, and that it has a theoretically meaningful link to predictors and criteria. When these assumptions fail, the partialled-out variance may be error or meaningful variance — so the supposed confound is not actually removed.

Controls cost degrees of freedom and statistical power

Every control consumes degrees of freedom and can reduce the explainable variance available in your outcome. A control related to the predictor or criterion can make a real relationship look null (or reversed), or make a non-existent relationship look real. Inclusion or exclusion can flip your substantive conclusions.

The residual-predictor problem: you may be studying something else

Once you control for variables, you are no longer studying the predictor — you are studying a residual predictor. In Breaugh's (2008) example, controlling for gender and weight left only ~40% of the original height variance, so “height” became something closer to physical proportionality. Concluding that “taller people earn more” is very different from “well-proportioned people earn more.”

Unreliable controls don't clean anything

If a control is measured unreliably, the variance you remove is contaminated with error, the confound remains, and regression weights become unstable. Controls used to rule out alternative explanations must be measured reliably — and their reliabilities should be reported.

The “conservative test” fallacy

Adding controls is often defended as a more conservative, rigorous, or stringent test. This was debunked decades ago (Meehl, 1971; Spector & Brannick, 2011). There is nothing inherently conservative or rigorous about including statistical controls.

“Reviewers will expect it” is not a justification

Anticipating reviewer or editor expectations is not a valid scientific rationale. The accumulated evidence supports declining unjustified controls and responding to such requests directly.

About this tool

This tool operationalizes the decision tree (Figure 2) and best-practice reporting recommendations from:

Bernerth, J. B., & Aguinis, H. (2016). A critical review and best-practice recommendations for control variable usage. Personnel Psychology, 69(1), 229–283.

It implements recommendations further developed in:

Aguinis, H. 2025. Research methodology: Best practices for rigorous, credible, and impactful research. SAGE. https://collegepublishing.sagepub.com/products/research-methodology-1-280088

How to use it

Work through one candidate control variable at a time in the Decision Tool. The wizard asks one question per screen and ends with a clear verdict (Include, Exclude, or Stop) plus the full reasoning chain. Evaluated variables collect in My Variables, feed the Methods Paragraph generator, and pair with the Reporting Checklist so nothing is left undocumented.

All data stays in your browser. Nothing is uploaded or transmitted. Use the export buttons to save a report or share with coauthors.
