Step 1 · Orientation

Why Bayesian?

You already know frequentist statistics. This section explains, in plain language, what Bayesian analysis gives you that p-values and confidence intervals cannot.

The core idea

Bayesian inference reallocates credibility across parameter values in light of the observed data. You start with a prior distribution over the possible values of your parameters, combine it with the data through the likelihood, and obtain a posterior distribution.

That posterior answers the question researchers actually care about:

p(hypothesis | data)  — how credible are the parameter values, given the data I collected?
Frequentist p-values instead give you p(data at least this extreme | null hypothesis true) — the probability of hypothetical data you never observed, assuming a hypothesis you are trying to evaluate.

Bayes' rule makes the relationship exact: p(θ|D) = p(D|θ)·p(θ) / p(D)  — posterior ∝ likelihood × prior.

Why p-values and CIs depend on intentions — but the posterior does not

A p-value is computed from the probability of data "as or more extreme" than what you observed, assuming the null is true and assuming a particular sampling and testing plan. That tail region depends on:

  • Stopping intention — did you plan to stop at a fixed N, stop at a deadline, or keep collecting until significance? Each defines a different space of "could-have-happened" datasets, so each yields a different p-value for the same data.
  • Testing intention — how many other tests were planned? Frequentist analysis must widen intervals and correct p-values for the comparisons you intended to make.

The Bayesian posterior & HDI depend only on the data you actually observed and the model and prior you declared. They do not change because you might have stopped at a different sample size or planned different comparisons. This is why the article says the posterior is "a fixed entity based on the observed data."

Step 3 · Prior

Determine & justify the prior

The prior must be explicitly specified and justified for a skeptical scientific audience — it cannot be set capriciously to favor your preferred result. When in doubt, use a broad, noncommittal prior so the data dominate.

Article defaults for standardized data. Intercept and each slope: Normal(mean = 0, SD = 10) — virtually flat over the plausible range of standardized coefficients (which fall roughly between −1 and +1). Residual noise σ: Uniform(0, 10) — extremely broad relative to the standardized noise of 1. These place essentially no bias on the posterior.

Step 6 · Advanced (optional)

Power & replication

In a Bayesian framework, power estimates are robust because they use the whole distribution of credible parameter values, not a single point estimate of effect size.

How Bayesian power works

Traditional power analysis fixes one target effect size and gives an estimate with "virtually unknowable" stability, because it ignores uncertainty in that effect. The Bayesian approach instead:

  1. Draws many parameter-value combinations from your posterior (each one a credible "true world").
  2. From each, simulates a fresh dataset of the size you plan to collect.
  3. Re-analyzes each simulated dataset and tallies whether the effect would be "detected" (e.g., its 95% HDI excludes 0 / falls outside the ROPE).
  4. The proportion detected across simulations is the estimated power — automatically carrying the full uncertainty of the posterior.

This works for any model regardless of hierarchical complexity or distributional assumptions, and the same machinery estimates replication probability.

Glossary

    

      
Prior
A probability distribution describing how plausible each parameter value is before seeing the new data. Can encode prior research or be deliberately broad/noncommittal.

      
Likelihood
The probability of the observed data given particular parameter values — your model of how the data were generated.

      
Posterior
The updated distribution over parameter values after combining prior and data via Bayes' rule. It is the complete Bayesian answer.

      
Credibility
Bayesian "probability" attached to parameter values; analysis reallocates credibility toward values consistent with the data.

      
MCMC
Markov chain Monte Carlo — an algorithm that draws a large representative sample from the posterior. More saved steps = sharper picture.

      
Burn-in
Early MCMC steps discarded so the chain can travel from its starting point into the bulk of the posterior before samples are kept.

      
HDI
Highest Density Interval — the range containing the 95% most credible values; every point inside has higher density than any point outside.

      
ROPE
Region Of Practical Equivalence — a band around a reference value (often 0) judged "close enough to be the same," enabling acceptance of a null, not just rejection.

      
Shrinkage
In hierarchical models, extreme group estimates are pulled toward the overall mean because groups share information — reducing overfitting.

      
R-hat
Gelman–Rubin convergence diagnostic; ≈ 1.00 indicates chains have converged to the same distribution.

      
Effective sample size (ESS)
The number of effectively independent draws after accounting for autocorrelation in the chain.
