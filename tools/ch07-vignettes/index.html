Welcome — let's design your EVM study

Experimental Vignette Methodology (EVM) presents participants with carefully constructed, realistic scenarios to assess outcomes such as intentions, attitudes, and behaviors. Done well, it resolves the classic dilemma between internal validity (experimental control over causes) and external validity (realism and generalizability). This tool guides you through every choice the method requires.

How this works You'll move through 3 stages and 10 decision points. Each gives you a plain-language explanation, captures your choice, lays out the trade-offs, and returns a recommendation that adapts to what you've already entered. A live calculator sizes your vignette set, and a recommendation engine keeps your analysis plan and design cautions in sync. At the end, generate a printable, copy-ready study plan.

Stage 1 · Planning — Decision Point 1

Is EVM a suitable approach?

EVM is powerful but not universal. It shines when you need experimental control to establish causation, when variables are known to correlate but causal direction is unclear, or when the topic is sensitive or impractical to manipulate in the field. It struggles when hypothetical scenarios cannot reproduce a genuinely high-stakes context. Answer the diagnostic below for a tailored verdict.

Learn more — when EVM fits, and when it doesn't
EVM is particularly useful when researchers need control over independent variables to gather causal evidence, letting you include factors relevant to the question while excluding confounds (Cavanaugh & Fritzsche, 1985). It is well suited to domains where variables are known to correlate but the nature and direction of causality are unclear, and to sensitive topics that cannot be ethically manipulated in the field — e.g., it would be unethical to manipulate workplace romances, yet Pierce, Aguinis, & Adams (2000) used vignettes to study attributions of blame when a romance becomes a harassment allegation.

The key caution: because participants respond to hypothetical scenarios, those scenarios may not recreate real-life pressures. In high-stakes decisions such as mergers and acquisitions, hypothetical presentation is unlikely to elicit the same responses as the real situation (Lohrke, Holloway, & Woolley, 2010).
Exemplar. Sauer (2011) used EVM to establish causal effects of leadership status and style — variables that correlate in the field but whose causal direction is hard to isolate without experimental control.
Stage 1 · Planning — Decision Point 2

Choose the type of EVM

There are two major types. Paper people studies assess explicit responses — decisions, judgments, attitudes participants are aware of and can report. Policy capturing / conjoint analysis captures implicit, real-time decision processes by having participants rank or choose among vignettes; it assumes the relevant factors are known a priori from theory. (Policy capturing and conjoint analysis are treated as a single type.)

Two quick questions to guide the choice

Learn more — explicit vs. implicit
Paper people present scenarios (typically written) and ask participants to make explicit decisions, judgments, choices, or state behavioral preferences. Widely used in leadership, executive behavior, entrepreneurship, OCB, and ethics. Best when the goal is to assess processes and outcomes participants are aware of and can report on.

Policy capturing / conjoint present scenarios with manipulated variables, then ask participants to make decisions between scenarios — capturing implicit, real-time processes often made without full awareness. Both assume the important decision factors are known a priori from theory, so they fit best in areas where theory clearly specifies relevant factors. They can extract a great deal of information from relatively few participants — valuable when the population is small or inaccessible.
Exemplars. Paper people: Raaijmakers et al. — managers read a new-law vignette manipulating two IVs, then reported compliance timing. Policy capturing: Skarlicki & Turner (2014) — participants rated 32 applicant profiles (5 IVs × 2 levels) to surface implicit fairness judgments. Conjoint: Shepherd, Patzelt, & Baron (2013) — 25 profiles probing moral disengagement in entrepreneurs' decisions.
Stage 1 · Planning — Decision Point 3

Choose the research design

In a between-person design each participant reads only one vignette and comparisons are made across people. In a within-person design each participant sees the same set of vignettes and comparisons are made within the person. In a mixed design different groups see different sets, but everyone within a group sees the same vignettes. The article's guidance: within-person or mixed are generally preferred; true between-person designs are uncommon and risk ungrounded judgments.

Required to ground judgments. Raaijmakers et al. gave every participant a general description of the issues before the manipulated vignette.
⚠ A between-person design without shared baseline context risks ungrounded, unreliable judgments.
Learn more — why between-person is risky
Because between-person participants see only a single vignette, they lose the comparison that helps ground responses contextually. Without other vignettes as referent points, responses may not reflect respondents' true judgments — true between-person designs are uncommon, especially when judgments are the dependent variable. If you must use one, provide participants with sufficient shared information to supply context. Within-person reveals manipulation effects inside one individual; mixed adds cross-respondent comparison because multiple people respond to the same vignettes.
Stage 1 · Planning — Decision Point 4

Choose the level of immersion

Immersion is the subjective experience of being personally inside the scenario. Moving up the ladder — text → pictures → audio → video → virtual reality — increases realism, engagement, recall, and external validity, and lets you control "natural noise" so scenarios feel lifelike without compromising internal validity. The cost: more time, money, and logistics (e.g., hiring actors). Pick a medium, then weigh realism against budget.

TextPicturesAudioVideoVirtual reality
Learn more — realism, "natural noise," and cost
Higher immersion engages participants' senses more fully, improving recall and approximating real-world experience — much as transfer of training improves when training resembles the job. Crucially, more lifelike scenarios carry more "natural noise"; when the researcher controls that noise, scenarios become realistic without sacrificing internal validity (e.g., Whiting et al. used equally attractive actors to rule out attractiveness effects). VR lets participants see, hear, and experience information impossible to convey in writing. The trade-off is cost and effort: a written vignette needs only a researcher's time, whereas Taggar & Neubert hired five professional actors for video. Fewer than 6% of reviewed studies used video.
Exemplar. Sauer (2011) used video vignettes completed online from participants' own environments — raising realism while preserving experimental control.

How will you set factor levels?

Learn more — orthogonal vs. actual derived cases
Attribute-driven (orthogonal) design: factors are set orthogonal to one another so you can cleanly assess each factor's independent effect. Limitation: some orthogonal combinations produce unrealistic scenarios — replace those with realistic ones to keep representation balanced.

Actual derived cases: levels are chosen to represent concrete values found in real settings, producing more lifelike scenarios and stronger generalizability. Recommendation: ask people similar to your target sample to supply realistic factor levels.

Either way, theory must justify factor selection — "the key is to identify what information is critical to the decision being studied" (Shepherd & Zacharakis, 1999).
Stage 1 · Planning — Decision Point 6

Choose the number of vignettes

Too many vignettes cause information overload and fatigue; too few bias responses and limit your ability to manipulate critical variables. The number is dictated by your purpose, but the factors and levels from Decision Point 5 set the math. Learn more — the rules behind the recommendation
First build the full population of vignettes (product of levels across factors). You need not use them all. For policy capturing / conjoint, use a minimum of 5 scenarios per manipulated attribute, and err toward more vignettes per respondent rather than fewer. To assess reliability, include 4–5 duplicated (repeated) vignettes so you can check whether respondents judge identical scenarios consistently. Balance these against Weber's (1992) warning that too many vignettes cause overload and fatigue while too few bias responses.
Stage 2 · Implementation — Decision Point 7

Specify the sample and number of participants

The quality of EVM data depends on the respondent. Match your sample to the population you want to generalize to, and make sure the scenarios are familiar to participants, or responses become artificial. Student samples are convenient but debated — they're especially problematic when the target is high-ranking or hard-to-reach (CEOs, board members, top management teams, early-stage entrepreneurs).

Enter the number of respondents you plan to recruit.
Learn more — samples, familiarity & eLancing
The more your respondents approximate the generalized population, the higher your external validity. Familiarity matters: if the situation is unfamiliar, responses are artificial — if you can't reach a suitable sample, you may need to adapt the vignettes to the sample's knowledge. An advantage of EVM is that it works outside the on-campus lab. eLancing (Internet freelancing marketplaces such as crowdsourcing platforms) gives access to large, diverse, global samples of working individuals at low cost — working samples can now be collected almost as easily as student samples, easing external-validity concerns. Within-person and mixed designs yield multiple observations per participant, so the number of data points exceeds the headcount.
Exemplar. Sauer (2011) recruited through a university's alumni database — over 80% non-students — a working sample far closer to the target population than undergraduates.
Stage 2 · Implementation — Decision Point 8

Choose the setting and timing for administration

Let participants respond in their natural setting (e.g., remotely from their own work environment) to boost realism and address the criticism that EVM lacks it. And complete the study in a single session when possible — multiple sessions are more vulnerable to history and other threats to validity.

Learn more — natural setting & single session
Allowing remote participation from a natural setting addresses the realism criticism, as in Sauer (2011), where participants responded from their own work environments rather than university facilities. On timing, a single session is best; some formats (e.g., long video vignettes) may require multiple sessions, but those are more likely to be affected by history and other validity threats — so minimize session count.
Stage 2 · Implementation — Decision Point 9

Choose the best method for analyzing the data

The right analysis follows directly from your design (Decision Point 3). The recommendation below updates automatically. Between-person designs use MANOVA / ANOVA / ANCOVA. Within-person and mixed designs have a two-level structure — vignettes nested within respondents — and call for multilevel modeling.

Learn more — why multilevel for within/mixed
Between-person designs are discouraged, but if used, MANOVA, ANOVA, and ANCOVA are appropriate. The preferred within-person and mixed designs produce a two-level data structure: the vignette level nested within the respondent level. Because the data are multilevel, analyses must address both levels simultaneously — hence the recommendation to use multilevel modeling (Aguinis, Gottfredson, & Culpepper, 2013; Hox et al., 1991), which correctly handles the non-independence of multiple vignette responses from the same person.
Stage 3 · Reporting — Decision Point 10

Choose how transparent to be in reporting

In the interest of replicability, describe the vignette creation and administration as transparently as possible, and disclose everything a reader needs to reproduce the study. Work through the checklist — it feeds directly into your final plan.

    Recommendation Include text or picture vignettes directly in the manuscript. Make video/VR materials available on request or via the journal website. Report the vignette creation and pilot-testing process, and disclose factors, levels, number of vignettes, duplicates, sample, setting, and analysis. Wherever possible, make your vignettes openly available — shared materials accelerate cumulative science.
    Learn more — transparency as replicability
    Management research is susceptible to credibility issues when procedures and results are withheld or inaccurately reported. Disclose as much as possible about the vignettes used: text/picture vignettes belong in the manuscript; video/VR materials should be available on request or via the journal site. Raaijmakers et al. included an appendix with both the vignettes and the process used to create and pilot-test them — giving future research a validated source to build on. The article recommends researchers make their vignettes openly available, much as the International Personality Item Pool freed personality items for cumulative use.

    The framework at a glance

    EVM resolves the trade-off between internal validity (experimental control to establish causation) and external validity (realism and generalizability) by presenting carefully constructed, realistic scenarios. The method spans three stages — Planning (DP 1–6), Implementation (DP 7–9), and Reporting (DP 10) — each with explicit trade-offs.

    This is a planning aid, not a substitute for the source articles or for methodological judgment. Recommendations adapt to your inputs but should be weighed against your study's specific goals and constraints.
